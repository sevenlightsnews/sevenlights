![Military base courthouse exterior at dawn, austere low building with a flagpole, barbed-wire perimeter fence and guard posts in the foreground, bare winter trees, heavy grey sky, somber stillness, editorial illustration, 3:2](/images/stories/hasan-seeks-stay-firing-squad-execution.webp)

Nidal Hasan, convicted in the 2009 mass shooting at Fort Hood, Texas, that killed 13 people, filed a motion in federal court Tuesday for a stay of his scheduled December execution by firing squad. His lawyers said he intends to mount a constitutional challenge to his death sentence.

The motion asks the court to pause proceedings so Hasan can submit a habeas corpus petition, a filing that requires the government to justify holding a prisoner. His lawyers said the petition will contest the lawfulness of a military execution.

"There is no question that the violence committed at Fort Hood caused unimaginable pain and loss," Anna Arceneaux, deputy director of the American Civil Liberties Union's Capital Punishment Project, said in a statement. "But the gravity of that loss does not change the government's obligation to uphold the principles that define our justice system. Hasan, like every person facing an irreversible punishment, must have a chance to pursue federal review of his case."

Hasan, 56, a former Army medical officer, had exhausted his appeals within the military justice system. That opened the way for President Trump to approve the death sentence in October, after which the Army set an execution date. The Pentagon's initial proposal to livestream the execution drew criticism from Democrats, Republicans and military officials, and the plan was dropped.

The case revived painful memories at the Army post, where a witness filing described the attack in detail this week. One witness told the court the building where Hasan opened fire had been crowded with soldiers returning from deployment and others preparing to leave. She said colleagues carried in the dying and wounded, and that a captain who rushed Hasan with a chair to stop the shooting was among the badly hurt. "The looks on the faces of my friends and colleagues as they carried in the dying and wounded that day … it will haunt me for the rest of my life," she said.

A stay would put the execution date in doubt while the habeas petition is litigated in federal court, a process that in capital cases commonly runs years. The government had not responded to the motion. No new execution date had been announced.
