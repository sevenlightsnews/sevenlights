President Trump called Thursday for an investigation into the government's economic statistics, asserting that growth figures released before the midterm elections had been understated "for political reasons." The Commerce Department had revised third-quarter gross domestic product growth to 4.3 percent annualized on Wednesday, from 3.4 percent in the first estimate.

In a post on Truth Social, Mr. Trump called the difference "a massive, and purposeful, error" and a "RECORD SETTING ERROR," and said the data would have been "three weeks late" if the revision had not come when it did. The Bureau of Economic Analysis published the third estimate on its scheduled release date.

The magnitude of the revision was not unusual. The bureau's first revisions of quarterly growth have averaged 0.9 percentage points over the past decade, against the 0.5-point average change in the third quarter. The bureau cited updated source data, including new software price information and revised retail sales figures, in raising the estimate. A revision of 1.2 percentage points was made to the second quarter of 2020, a larger move than this week's.

The episode follows the president's dismissal of the commissioner of labor statistics last year. That action drew objections from economists and former officials of both parties, who held that the independence of federal statistics underpins market confidence and the conduct of monetary policy. Economists said the growth figure matters directly to the Federal Reserve, which weighs output and inflation data in setting interest rates; a stronger third quarter reduces the case for near-term rate cuts.

The president's post did not say which agencies would be investigated or by whom, and there was no immediate indication that any agency had opened an inquiry. The next quarterly estimate of growth, for the fourth quarter, is scheduled for December 23.

The administration has said its pressure on the agencies seeks accuracy. Career statisticians and private economists have said the revision cycle, first estimates and successive updates are a routine feature of national accounting, in which early figures rest on incomplete source data.
