The U.S. Army will detonate about five tons of lead azide, a primary explosive used to set off bombs and shells, in an open blast at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown on Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving. The schedule announced Sunday night moved the detonation from Friday, then to Monday, and finally to Wednesday to allow for weather conditions and mitigation measures.

The material has been stored for more than 20 years in underground concrete tanks that have since dried out. In a letter to Governor Kim Reynolds on Friday, regional Environmental Protection Agency administrator Jim Macy wrote that the Army had documented recrystallization of the lead azide, raising the risk of an accidental explosion. An administration court filing said the compound "is now so sensitive to temperature, friction and shock that it cannot be safely moved." It cannot be opened or treated without a significant risk of explosion, the filing said.

President Trump ordered the Army on Monday to pause the blast pending approval from a federal appeals court and from Democratic Gov.-elect Rob Sand. Mr. Trump warned in a Truth Social post that he would hold Mr. Sand responsible for "any and all death or destruction" if the governor-elect withheld approval.

Earthjustice sued last week to halt the detonation on behalf of the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter. A federal appeals court subsequently paused an EPA emergency permit authorizing the blast. The administration argued in a Sunday filing to the Eighth Circuit that the Army could still proceed with a controlled detonation with the permit stayed; the court allowed the administration to move forward.

State officials said last week they had learned of the plan only shortly before it was announced. Mr. Sand and Senator-elect Josh Turek condemned the Army's communications as delayed and inadequate. Senator Tammy Duckworth of Illinois called the episode "an extreme failure of communication, planning and leadership."

The Army has advised residents near the plant to avoid the area, stay indoors and clean surfaces after it declares the airborne exposure risk over. The American Lung Association warned that residents of eastern Iowa and western Illinois could be exposed to lead.

The detonation is set for Wednesday. Officials have said the blast cannot be deferred indefinitely.

**Update, November 27:** The Army carried out the detonation on Wednesday, destroying 68 drums holding about 9,700 pounds of degraded lead azide at the plant in Middletown. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said the controlled detonation "exceeded all expectations and was a HUGE success," and that a federal sensor array across the area showed no readings above 0.0. President Trump said the mitigation efforts "far exceeded expectations." Residents within a five-mile cone southwest of the plant had been evacuated and were to be notified by phone when it was safe to return; others were told to shelter in place, close windows and doors and take precautions for children and livestock.

**Update, November 28:** Environmental Protection Agency Administrator Lee Zeldin said air monitors detected zero airborne lead after the blast and that more than 1,000 post-detonation samples had been taken from homes, parks, farms and drinking water taps, with laboratory results projected within 24 to 48 hours. Sampling of churches, schools and playgrounds continued. The Federal Emergency Management Agency issued the all-clear around the plant, and FEMA administrator Cameron Hamilton said additional resources and testing would follow in the coming days. Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, commanding general of the Army Sustainment Command, said in a video message that all the lead azide barrels had been destroyed, that there were no secondary explosions and no infrastructure damage. Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker said his state was "seeing little to no impact" and kept state agencies on standby. Governor-elect Rob Sand demanded transparency over the "chaotic and unclear" lead-up and said any affected Iowans "must be taken care of fully, right away." Independent scientists cautioned that real-time monitors alone cannot settle how much lead was deposited; physical analysis of samples will take longer.
