Lana and Lilly Wachowski have notified Warner Bros. that they are exercising copyright termination rights over The Matrix, a move that could return the franchise's film rights to its creators by February 2029 and throw into question the planned fifth film that Warner Bros.' new owners had been developing.

The notice was first reported by Puck's Eriq Gardner. Under United States copyright law, a creator may terminate a grant of rights 35 years after it was made, provided a notice of termination is served on the rights holder between two and ten years before the effective date. Now that the notice has been filed, the reversion occurs automatically on the termination date unless the parties reach a new agreement, and Gardner wrote that Skydance, which acquired Warner Bros. earlier this fall, is legally obliged to acknowledge the claim.

Skydance's acquisition made it steward of a shelf of juggernaut franchises. Holding Neo and Trinity may prove harder than holding Frodo: the termination route is available precisely because Congress gave authors a second bite against old contracts.

Warner Bros. has legal openings. Gardner noted the studio could argue the Wachowskis wrote the first Matrix film under an older contract with the former executive Lorenzo di Bonaventura — covering the spec script for "Assassins," the 1996 film "Bound" and The Matrix itself. That would make the film a "work for hire," ineligible for termination. Yet in earlier litigation Warner Bros. said the Wachowskis began work on The Matrix as early as 1992, predating the di Bonaventura deal and undercutting that argument.

The likelier outcome is a settlement. Skydance renegotiated a new grant of rights to "The Godfather" with the estate of Mario Puzo last year rather than fight termination. Gardner wrote that the Wachowskis have been "encouraging" of the sequel concept drawn up for director Drew Goddard and have "even increased their involvement" with it. Lana Wachowski was signed on to produce. Lilly Wachowski said in September she did not want to "play in that sandbox anymore," having passed on "The Matrix Resurrections."

The Wachowskis are not alone in testing the statute. Stephen King and Mike Judge have filed similar notices over "The Shawshank Redemption" and "Beavis and Butt-Head," respectively.

Skydance is optimistic a deal can be struck, Puck reported. The Goddard film has no start date.
