![Empty departure hall at a large airport under cold fluorescent light, rows of dark flight-information screens, a handful of passengers with bags standing before a gate agent desk, quiet and still, photorealistic, 3:2](/images/stories/faa-nationwide-ground-stop-routers.webp)

The Federal Aviation Administration halted every departure in the United States on Friday afternoon and held aircraft on the ground for roughly four hours, an interruption Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy attributed to overloaded routers in an aging air traffic control system. The agency reopened the airspace at 8:15 p.m. Eastern. Aviation tracker FlightAware counted more than 11,000 flights delayed or canceled by 9 p.m., including more than 6,000 cancellations.

The FAA issued the ground stop after communication failures struck multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers, the facilities that direct high-altitude traffic between airports. The agency cited "extraordinary circumstances" and "reasons of safety" and said technicians were investigating.

Duffy said the government had identified the problem. "Our routers actually got overloaded with data, and … they started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers," he said at a White House briefing. "And because of that, we shut the system down." He added that the system "is operational right now."

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said preliminary investigations found no link to an attack. "It's just a telecommunications outage as of right now," he said. Officials are examining incompatibilities between older equipment and newer technology now being installed. The FAA has been rolling out an artificial intelligence tool to manage airspace congestion; its developer denied any connection to Friday's failure.

The order caught aviation without warning. Passengers already aboard aircraft sat on tarmac for hours, and terminal gates filled as travelers waited for word. Airlines planned around the disruption through the weekend, with planes, crews and passengers out of position. College and professional teams scrambled to rebook charter travel ahead of weekend games.

Duffy used the briefing to press Congress for money to replace the control system, saying the government could not "fully fund a full build of a brand new air traffic control system" today and warning that similar failures could recur without it. "I can't build things if I don't have money," he said. Lawmakers including California Governor Gavin Newsom demanded answers about the outage and the condition of the system.

The FAA said late Friday that the cause remained under review. The agency declared the system operational, and carriers began working through the backlog of stranded passengers and aircraft through Saturday.
