Shareholders sued Nvidia and its chief executive, Jensen Huang, in federal court in California on Tuesday, accusing the company and its founder of misleading investors about a Chinese spying scandal that has engulfed the world's most valuable chipmaker. Nvidia shares fell on Monday, closing down 4.3 per cent and cutting the company's market value by roughly $190bn.

The complaint alleges that Nvidia and Mr. Huang failed to disclose that the company's dealings with Alice Huang, a former executive of Singapore-based Megaspeed, raised national security concerns that threatened the chipmaker's reputation and business prospects. The New York Times reported the alleged relationship last month and reported that US authorities had concluded Ms. Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset. She is not related to Mr. Huang.

The lawsuit claims a statement Nvidia gave the Times last month — that Mr. Huang's interactions with Ms. Huang were "not unusual or unexpected," and that "meetings with customers are part of a CEO's job; Jensen talks with thousands of customer executives every year" — was "materially false and misleading at the time it was made because Jensen Huang had an affair with Alice Huang." The complaint also accuses the company of false statements about its relationship with Megaspeed, which has reportedly been investigated over suspicions it diverted Nvidia chips to China in breach of US export restrictions.

President Trump said this week the Federal Bureau of Investigation was examining Mr. Huang's affair with the alleged spy. Mr. Huang told Nvidia employees on Sunday that he had been "unfaithful" to his wife, without naming the other party. The company announced Sunday that it had begun an independent investigation into reports that Mr. Huang was the "target of a foreign intelligence effort."

On the Megaspeed inquiry, Mr. Huang said in an interview published on 30 October: "We've studied all their deployments of our GPUs and counted every one. And I think the US government also has as well." Megaspeed said last year it had acted "fully in compliance with all applicable laws, including US export control regulations."

Fast Company reported that current and former Washington technology officials confirmed the seriousness of the matter, and that legal scholars consider a securities-fraud claim the most clear-cut exposure the company now faces. The suit was filed five minutes after the market closed on Monday.

Nvidia and Megaspeed did not immediately respond to requests for comment. Mr. Huang co-founded the company in 1993 and has led it since. The company remains subject to the board's independent review, with no findings announced.
