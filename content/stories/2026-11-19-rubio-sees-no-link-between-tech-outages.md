There is "zero evidence" that the wave of technology outages that has grounded flights, cut off work access, limited banking and disrupted classified communications is linked or the work of hostile actors, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Wednesday.

Speaking to reporters at the Solaire Resort near Manila, Philippines, Rubio said the United States was "not under systemic attack" and that the cascade of network problems was "not inter-related." He added: "We have zero evidence or any reason to believe that any of these isolated or individual instances have anything to do with nefarious actors, with cyber-hackers, with criminals, with another nation state, with a malign actor of any kind."

Microsoft, major US banks, Apple, American air traffic control systems and the National Security Agency have all been affected by failures and outages in recent days. On Wednesday, Intel officials were questioned by lawmakers in Washington over the disruptions.

The director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, attributed the incidents to security upgrades that ran long or met unexpected problems. "Everybody recognises it's time to upgrade their systems, and as they upgrade them, they're not coming back as expected in all cases, and in some cases systems have to hand off to other systems, and those handoffs are not coming back as expected," he told Fox Business.

Rubio's remarks coincided with the disclosure by the US security firm Calif of a vulnerability in Java, a programming language used by 90 per cent of Fortune 500 companies. Calif, whose researchers used AI tools from OpenAI to find the flaw, said it could allow hackers to execute code remotely and plant malicious software on target systems. "Java is the beating heart of the modern economy," the firm said, citing banking, aviation and telecommunications. Calif recently found similar glitches in PHP.

The volume of discovered flaws has risen sharply since April, when the AI company Anthropic released its Mythos model, notable for identifying information-technology weaknesses; vulnerabilities found by leading technology companies have increased eightfold since. OpenAI has also released models capable of finding previously unseen flaws.

Tal Be'ery, a cybersecurity expert, said he did not believe the incidents were directly linked but shared an underlying theme: artificial intelligence. Systems are buckling under the pressure of adding AI or patching flaws that AI discovers quickly, he said: "Our IT infrastructure is brittle."

Officials have ordered reviews of the upgrade programmes. No criminal or foreign involvement has been established.
