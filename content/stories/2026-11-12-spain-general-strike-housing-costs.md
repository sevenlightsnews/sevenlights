Millions of workers joined Spain's first nationwide general strike in 14 years on Wednesday, a 24-hour stoppage over soaring housing costs staged weeks before a general election in which the far right could enter government.

The strike was called singularly by tenants' associations and later backed by workers' unions. Unions reported participation of 90 per cent in the automotive and port sectors and 82 per cent in waste collection, though support varied across the country. The strongest response came in coastal regions such as Catalonia and Andalucía, where housing costs have risen most sharply. Many schools and universities closed or operated at minimal capacity, and public transport was disrupted in several cities.

Sandra Mora of the Madrid Tenants' Union told state broadcaster RNE that workers who are also renters or mortgage holders were "the ones with the power to halt production and bring the economy to a standstill." The Confederation of Tenants' Association called the strike a success, claiming about four million participants by midday.

Marches drew large crowds despite pouring rain in Madrid, where the government estimated 120,000 demonstrators. In Barcelona, unions claimed 400,000 marchers and local police put the figure at 55,000. After a peaceful march against rising rents in the Catalan capital, small groups of hooded protesters vandalised shops, set fire to rubbish bins and burning tyres and clashed with police. Scuffles also broke out between pickets and police near Madrid's main wholesale market. Government officials said more than 50 people were arrested over minor incidents nationwide.

The Madrid action reinforced a teachers' strike that began nearly a month ago over working conditions. Spain's meteorological agency issued 14 alerts for two storms, casting doubt over some afternoon marches.

The stoppage grew out of a housing movement that gathered force after the eviction in September of Maricarmen, an 87-year-old woman whose death shortly afterward prompted encampments in Madrid and other cities. Campaigners said the networks built over years supplied the strike's organisation.

The election, due within weeks, has made housing the dominant domestic issue. Unions and tenant groups have pressed the parties to commit to rent controls and new public housing, and the strike tested whether that pressure could be carried into the campaign. No further nationwide stoppage was announced.
