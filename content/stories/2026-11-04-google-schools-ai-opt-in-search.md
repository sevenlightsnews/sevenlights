Google announced on Tuesday that it will disable its artificial-intelligence products for K-12 student accounts next year unless school administrators explicitly turn them on, a reversal of the automatic enablement it introduced in Google Classroom in August. Schools that decline the AI tools will lose something larger: access to Google Search itself.

The change runs across Gemini, NotebookLM, Gemini in Classroom and Search. Because AI features are now integrated into the search engine, they cannot be switched off separately. "Search and AI are deeply integrated," Chris Phillips, vice president for education at Google, told The New York Times. A school that restricts AI altogether will find that students "will not have access to search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available," he said.

Mr Phillips wrote in a blog post that further specifics would come in the coming months, ahead of a rollout intended to give districts "time to prepare and enable a smooth transition." The stated aim was "to provide school leaders the control and confidence to use AI products in classrooms as they see fit."

Google made the announcement alongside the launch in schools of Guided Learning, a tool unveiled earlier this year that works as a tutor, intended to build understanding rather than supply direct answers. The release arrives amid a standing argument among educators that chatbots short-circuit the learning process by producing answers on demand.

The reversal follows pressure from outside groups. In October, technology and child-safety organisations wrote to some 150 United States school districts and their leadership bodies asking them to disable Gemini on school devices, concluding that "children are at risk of being harmed."

Researchers called the Search linkage the consequential part. Ying Xu, an assistant professor at the Harvard Graduate School of Education who directs the Harvard Child-Centered AI Lab, said traditional searching and AI querying exercise different skills, from framing a query to weighing sources. Even with Guided Learning, she said, "students may be losing opportunities to practice these important information-seeking skills." She questioned whether schools will retain any option for search without AI features, and warned that better-resourced districts will handle these decisions more easily than others.

Given the reach of Google's education suite and of Chromebooks, the decision leaves districts to weigh instruction against infrastructure. Google has said details for administrators will follow before the changes take effect.
