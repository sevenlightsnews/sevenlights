Norse Atlantic Airways filed for bankruptcy in a Norwegian court Friday, ending its attempt to operate a low-cost long-haul network between Europe and North America. The airline had warned in recent days that a filing was certain after it failed to attract new owners or fresh capital.

The carrier had suspended all flights in October when a financing round collapsed, leaving it unable to cover lease payments, fuel and staffing. Its last departures took place earlier this week, and the company said remaining staff would be handled under Norwegian creditor procedures. The administrator will review the estate, including the airline's leased Boeing 787 fleet.

Norse was founded in 2021 and began flying in 2022, betting that discount fares on wide-body jets could win transatlantic leisure traffic from network carriers. The company expanded routes between Britain, continental Europe, the United States and Scandinavia, then contracted sharply in 2023 when engine inspections grounded part of its 787 fleet. It returned aircraft to service and rebuilt schedules through 2024 and 2025.

The airline's finances never stabilized. Rival low-cost operators moved onto transatlantic routes from Ireland and Iceland with lower cost bases, while fuel, insurance and aircraft-lease costs rose. Norse sought strategic investors repeatedly through the autumn; court filings and statements said the company required a substantial capital injection to resume operations and had none in hand.

The bankruptcy removes one of the more closely watched startup experiments in European aviation. Norwegian Air Shuttle, the low-cost pioneer that once flew transatlantic routes, survives after its own restructuring; competitors including Icelandair, Play's successors and the network airlines now hold the displaced traffic. European regulators had granted Norse slots at several congested airports, which will be returned or reassessed.

Passengers holding unflown tickets are covered under standard insolvency and consumer-protection arrangements in the countries of departure. The company said ticket refunds would be processed through those schemes rather than by the airline.

The court's next steps include appointing an estate administrator and setting a creditor meeting. No process for selling the airline's remaining assets, including its slots and spares, was announced Friday.
