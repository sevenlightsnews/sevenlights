The United States announced on Wednesday that it is proposing new tariffs on 14 trading partners: China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia and Bangladesh. The move presses ahead with President Trump's trade program on a fresh legal footing.

For China, the measure would add 20 per cent to imports, bringing the total burden to about 40 per cent, though some rates would be effectively reduced by caps the administration has already negotiated with individual governments.

The legal basis is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which lets the executive branch answer unfair foreign trade practices after formal investigations. The administration opened those inquiries into industrial subsidies and structural excess capacity in March, weeks after the Supreme Court ruled in February that the Liberation Day tariffs had been improperly imposed under an emergency powers law. The court's narrow decision did not curb the president's authority to tariff under other statutes.

"For decades, many of our trading partners have adopted industrial strategies built on targeting manufacturing, wage and labor suppression, financial and other support, non-market economy inputs, and other market distorting practices that tilt the trade balance in their favor while eroding America's manufacturing base," the US trade representative, Jamieson Greer, said in a statement. "President Trump is proposing to take action to bring about the elimination of these unfair acts." Greer said the duties would "level the playing field for American workers."

The office will hold hearings on the proposals on January 25. The White House gave no firm timeline, saying the tariffs could take effect in January at the earliest; Greer told CNBC they likely would not be in force for months, as the measure must pass public consultation.

This is the second large use of Section 301. In July the White House announced a broad slate of tariffs on more than 80 countries, including Britain, Mexico and Canada, over failures to enforce bans on forced labor. A lawsuit over that batch awaits a decision at the Court of International Trade, whose outcome could bear on the new round.

Economists warned that tariffs fuel inflation and lift consumer prices when importers pass on costs. Shipping and retail groups had no immediate comment on the proposal.

The proposals were published with extensive reports on how each jurisdiction's practices purportedly affect American commerce. A final decision would come after the hearings.
