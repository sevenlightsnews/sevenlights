![Stacks of redacted legal documents with black marker bars across typed pages on a wooden counsel table, a gavel resting beside them, dim courtroom light, shallow depth of field, no readable text, editorial photojournalism photograph, 3:2](/images/stories/omar-home-search-records-unsealed.webp)

A federal judge released more than 160 pages of court records Monday night detailing how the Justice Department and the courts debated the constitutional implications of a search of the Washington home of Representative Ilhan Omar of Minnesota. The documents do not include the search warrant itself.

Chief U.S. District Judge James E. Boasberg ruled that portions of the sealed records be made public, with redactions, after a coalition of news publications including NBCUniversal News Group sought access. Judge Boasberg agreed with the Justice Department that most of the records should remain sealed, but said an exchange over the protection of Ms. Omar's "speech or debate" rights — the constitutional clause shielding lawmakers from liability tied to legislative acts — should be released.

The unredacted passages show the length to which a magistrate judge worked to satisfy himself that the Trump administration followed proper procedures in obtaining a warrant to search a member of Congress's home. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh asked the Justice Department several times for briefings explaining its theory of probable cause, and the department said the process included "several rounds" of affidavit revisions. Judge Sharbaugh ultimately found the speech or debate clause at least partly relevant because the target was a legislator's home, and "special search procedures" were carried out during the search, according to the records.

The list of property the government planned to seize is largely redacted, and the list of what agents actually took is entirely redacted. Unredacted passages indicate the warrant covered "all records and information that constitute fruits, contraband, evidence, and instrumentalities" of violations of an offense that is withheld, along with papers, photographs, thumb drives, external hard drives and other electronic media holding such material.

About half of the released pages are mostly or entirely redacted, and the government's probable-cause theory remains largely hidden from public view. The Justice Department has argued that Judge Boasberg's unsealing decision runs against his own earlier ruling in a separate matter involving the president.

Further records remain sealed, and the underlying investigation continues.
