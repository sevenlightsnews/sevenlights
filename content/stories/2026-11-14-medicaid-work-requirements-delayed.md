The Trump administration announced Friday a six-month delay in enforcing the Medicaid work requirements scheduled to take effect in January, giving states an optional "hold harmless" period through June 30. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services said the window would let states modernize systems and streamline the applicant experience.

Under the rules, enacted in last year's budget law, adults without dependent children must work, attend school or volunteer at least 80 hours a month, or claim an exemption, to keep coverage. States that opt into the delay will still begin checking compliance on Jan. 1, but no one will lose coverage for failing the requirements until July.

The pause follows months of warnings from state officials and health groups that the rollout was moving too fast. A federal judge last month blocked enforcement of the portion of the rule governing medically frail recipients, ruling that states lacked adequate time and resources to overhaul eligibility systems. Nebraska's early start on May 1 produced confusion and coverage denials over unfiled paperwork; state figures through August showed more than 1,000 people had lost coverage or been denied benefits. Five Democratic governors asked Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. last month to postpone the policy.

The Congressional Budget Office estimated the work requirements alone would leave 5.3 million more people uninsured by 2034 while saving the federal government $317 billion, part of nearly $1 trillion in Medicaid cuts in the law. The administration argues the rules encourage work and protect benefit programs for those who need them.

"States have made real progress, and they've been clear with us about what it takes to get this right for beneficiaries," said Dan Brillman, deputy administrator and director of the Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services.

The delay comes a little more than a week after Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress on a pledge to reverse the law's health and safety-net cuts. Democrats intend to press for further delays or to repeal the requirements outright, setting up a confrontation over a provision that remains technically in force. State systems begin their first compliance checks in six weeks.
