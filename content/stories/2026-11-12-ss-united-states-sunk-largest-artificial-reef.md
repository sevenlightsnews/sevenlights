The SS United States, once the fastest ocean liner in the world, was sunk off Destin, Florida, on Wednesday to become the world's largest artificial reef, closing the last chapter of a ship that had sat decaying for years after preservation efforts failed.

Crews deployed the vessel at about 2:40 p.m. ET, and the sinking began shortly before 5 p.m. about 20 nautical miles off the Gulf Coast between the Destin-Fort Walton Beach region and Pensacola. The operation was controlled and used no explosives, and the ship settled to the ocean floor over several hours, concluding later in the evening.

The nearly 1,000-foot American-made ship was launched in 1952 and set a transatlantic speed record on its maiden voyage, a record that still stands for passenger liners. It carried thousands of passengers across the Atlantic over 17 years of service before its retirement in 1969. In the weeks before the sinking it was hollowed out and openings were cut into its hull so the sea would enter quickly and predictably.

The wreck will rest upright on the seabed and is expected to open as a diving destination once marine life establishes itself on the decks and funnels. The Destin-Fort Walton Beach metropolitan area says it already has about 580 artificial reefs off its coastline, the most of any region in the country; the United States will dwarf every one of them.

The liner's end follows a long search for a purpose. Proposals to convert it into a museum or hotel in Philadelphia and elsewhere collapsed one after another, and the ship changed hands several times as storage costs mounted. A federal review ultimately cleared the way for sinking, with conditions covering environmental testing and the removal of materials judged hazardous.

For the maritime community the scuttling settled a recurring argument over whether the ship could have been saved. Its advocates argued the hull embodied mid-century American engineering; its final owners said no preservation plan ever came with the money to match it.

The reef's operators expect dive permits to be issued after surveys confirm the wreck's stability. No memorial service on the water has been announced, though liner societies had planned gatherings around the date.
