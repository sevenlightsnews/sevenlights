![Symmetrical view down the central aisle of an ornate, nearly empty senate-style legislative chamber with curved rows of wooden desks, toward a lone back-turned figure standing at a podium beneath flags and a glowing coffered ceiling.](/images/stories/senate-rejects-iran-war-powers-resolution.webp)

The Senate rejected a war powers resolution on Thursday, 48 to 51, that would have directed President Trump to remove American forces from hostilities with Iran. It was the first such vote in either chamber since the midterm elections of November 3, in which the president's party lost control of both houses of Congress.

Senators Susan Collins of Maine, Rand Paul of Kentucky and Lisa Murkowski of Alaska joined every Senate Democrat except John Fetterman of Pennsylvania, who voted against. Democrats have introduced a steady stream of war powers measures since the war began in February with a joint United States-Israel attack on Iran. More than a dozen votes have followed; most fell short. One passed both chambers in June, only for Mr. Trump to ignore it.

Senator Tim Kaine of Virginia, who has led the Democratic efforts, said before the vote that the election had settled the question of public opinion. "A historic repudiation of the party holding onto the White House, with much of that energy driven by Americans' deep dissatisfaction with war, waged unilaterally by a president who said he would not, causing costs to dramatically rise on every American family," Kaine said. Gasoline and diesel prices have climbed during the conflict, and voters cited the war among their top concerns at the polls.

Republican support eroded but did not break. Thom Tillis of North Carolina, retiring in January after backing earlier resolutions, voted against this one: "This one doesn't go to the president, so it's just a statement from Congress." Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, also outgoing and crucial to the June measure's passage, opposed it. Senator Chris Murphy was absent after a recent stroke. After the June vote, Mr. Trump had lashed out at the Republicans who defected at a testy lunch meeting with senators, and a subsequent war powers effort fell short when key Republicans shifted.

The resolution had cleared the House in September with all House Democrats and seven Republicans, several of whom lost re-election. Its practical effect would have been limited in any case: under the 1973 War Powers Resolution a president must withdraw forces if Congress so directs by concurrent resolution, but administrations of both parties have called that provision unconstitutional, and the White House had challenged the measure's validity.

Democrats signaled they would keep forcing votes. The new Congress convenes in January with Democrats holding the House; the Senate remains in Republican hands by a single seat.
