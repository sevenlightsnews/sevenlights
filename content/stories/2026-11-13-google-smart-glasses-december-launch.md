![Modern eyeglasses with thin frames on a minimalist display stand in a bright showroom, small camera module on the left temple and a tiny illuminated indicator on the frame front, soft studio light, blurred glass cases behind, photorealistic, 3:2](/images/stories/google-smart-glasses-december-launch.webp)

Google said its first glasses running Android XR, developed with Samsung, Warby Parker and Gentle Monster, go on sale December 10, and that preorders are open. Warby Parker frames start at $395 and Gentle Monster frames at $449.

The eyewear carries no display. It is built around Gemini: speakers, four beamforming microphones and a camera on the left temple, with a small light to signal when recording. The frames weigh about 40 grams.

Google is pitching privacy as the headline feature, an apparent answer to the objections that ended Google Glass in 2015. Shahram Izadi, head of the company's extended-reality division, said the glasses' protections go further than any previous Google product. Camera imagery is not saved to users' Gemini history and data is encrypted. The glasses detect tampering and block capture if the recording light is covered. Before imagery is sent to Gemini, the faces and body parts of bystanders are obscured. The microphones default to capturing the wearer's voice, and the terms of service prohibit recording bystanders without permission. Accounts require users to be at least 18.

Google said it will ban accessories that hide the recording light, restrict Shopping and Ads functions and limit what may be captured for YouTube, and plans a private-compute tier that keeps processing on the device.

The company has opened showrooms in New York and other cities where buyers can try the frames before ordering. Further styles from additional eyewear partners are planned.

The device arrives a decade after Snap's Spectacles and a decade after Google Glass was ridiculed and abandoned. Google is betting that privacy engineering can change the reception. Privacy advocates were not persuaded. Reem Suleiman of the digital-rights group Fight for the Future said camera-equipped glasses should not be normalized in public spaces. Juston Payne, who oversees extended-reality products at Google, said the company had weighed privacy questions throughout the design process.
