![At golden-hour dusk a dense crowd seen from behind raises its hands toward a large brightly lit outdoor projection screen displaying close-up generic portraits, with rows of white A-frame tents on a hillside and mountain ridges under a hazy orange-pink sky.](/images/stories/icj-rules-myanmar-genocide-rohingya.webp)

The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar's security forces committed genocide against the Rohingya minority in the 2017 campaign that killed about 10,000 people and drove more than 700,000 across the border into Bangladesh. It is the first time the United Nations' top court has held a state responsible for genocide.

The case was brought in 2019 by The Gambia under the Genocide Convention, on behalf of the stateless minority. In the courtroom at The Hague there were tears; in the refugee camps of Cox's Bazar, where the verdict was translated into the Rohingya language for crowds gathered before specially installed screens, the reaction was applause and cheering, with many holding signs reading "Thank You Gambia" and "Justice = Safe Return."

"The victory belongs to the Rohingya. They survived. They testified. And today, they will believe," said Dawda Jallow, The Gambia's justice minister, speaking at the court. Salma, a 30-year-old survivor who lost family members in 2017, said: "Everyone worked hard and struggled for this justice, for this cause. When they hear it today, everyone will be happy, thank God."

The court ordered Myanmar to allow the "safe and dignified return to their place of residence and the reunification of families" for Rohingya displaced by what it called the country's wrongful acts, and held that Myanmar must compensate the Rohingya, with the terms to be determined at a later date. The orders are legally binding, but the court has no power of its own to enforce them; what follows rests with governments around the world.

Myanmar's government had argued the campaign in Rakhine state was a legitimate response to guerrilla attacks by a Rohingya militant group, said there was no direct evidence of genocidal intent, and insisted the Rohingya had burned their own homes. Survivors described massacres, widespread rape, arson and looting during the army's "clearance operations."

The judgment closes a decade-long effort at legal recognition but arrives amid continued violence. A military coup in 2021 plunged Myanmar into civil war, and a recent wave of attacks by the Myanmar military has driven new displacement, including among Rohingya who had remained in the country. Immigration lawyers and community leaders in Australia, which has pledged more than one billion dollars in humanitarian assistance since 2017, welcomed the ruling as long-overdue recognition and called on governments to translate it into diplomatic pressure and support for safe, voluntary return.
