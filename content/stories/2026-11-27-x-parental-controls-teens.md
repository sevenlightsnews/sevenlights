X, the social network formerly known as Twitter, has added parental controls for the first time in its 20-year history, giving parents and guardians of users aged 13 to 17 tools to manage what their teens see and with whom they interact.

Accounts belonging to teenagers will be set to "protected mode" by default, the company said. In that setting, users cannot see sensitive content and are not served targeted advertisements.

The additional controls let adults see how much time a teen spends on the platform each week and set daily limits. They also require approval for new followers; limit "For You" recommendations to accounts the teen already follows; filter specific words; turn off sensitive media; specify who may send direct messages; and show which accounts the teen has corresponded with one-on-one. Parents may supervise up to five teen accounts.

The system stops short of surveillance. Parents and guardians cannot read private messages, see searches or viewing history, post as their teen or take over the account. Teenagers can see what restrictions have been applied to their accounts. Linking starts with the parent or guardian and finishes with the teen, X said: "Both users have to agree."

The controls arrive months after X came under fire for a lack of safeguards preventing its AI chatbot from being used to generate sexualized images of children and women. The network has also long contended with child sexual abuse material, misinformation and AI bots, and openly permits adult content. X has generally not been a platform aimed at teenagers, but a 2024 study found that nearly a third of Generation Alpha children hold an X account. The service requires users to be at least 13 years old.

The company framed the change as a balance between supervision and privacy. "Parents want practical tools, and teens deserve room to grow," X said in announcing the feature.

The move brings X broadly in line with rival networks, most of which have offered guardian controls for years, though X's design leaves teens a measure of control over their own accounts that many competitors do not. Regulators in the United States and abroad have pressed social platforms over child safety, and several have moved to require age verification or parental consent for underage users.

The controls take effect on a platform where teenagers remain a minority of users and where the company has historically resisted content restrictions of every kind.
