The White House counsel warned Representative Robert Garcia this week that aggressive congressional oversight of the Trump administration "may cross the line into blatant criminality." The incoming Democratic chairman of the House Oversight Committee turned the letter into a political counterattack, saying it indicated the president has "something to hide."

Garcia, the Washington Democrat in line to lead the oversight panel when Democrats take the House in January, said the threat of criminal investigations into lawmakers for doing their oversight work was itself evidence of vulnerability. "It appears Donald Trump has something to hide," he said.

The letter responds to Democratic pledges to investigate senior officials over the administration's conduct, a scrutiny the White House now warns could become unlawful. It represents the most explicit warning yet from the president's lawyers to incoming committee chairs.

Devlin Barrett of the New York Times, discussing the letter on the NewsHour, said that "as a legal document, it doesn't mean very much," but that as a political document it was "quite important." It telegraphed how hard the White House intends to fight the coming inquiries. The administration has already fought judicial inquiries, and officials have signalled that subpoenas would be met with privilege claims and litigation.

The warning arrived alongside a wave of high-profile resignations at the Justice Department following last week's Democratic election victories, with reporting that more departures are expected. Career prosecutors who worked on matters now likely to be examined by Congress are among those leaving.

Republicans defended the letter as a lawful reminder that oversight has boundaries. The White House did not identify the specific oversight plans it considered criminal, and legal scholars noted that Congress's investigative powers are broad and that threatening members over their committee work has no clear statutory basis.

The exchange set the tone for the new Congress. Garcia vowed to press on in the face of what he called "baseless threats," and White House lawyers signalled that congressional inquiries would be met with objections and litigation. A confrontation over documents and testimony now appears certain.
