![Vast crowd filling a hilltop park overlooking a Central European city, demonstrators in pointed paper hats stretching to the horizon under overcast autumn light, river and spires in the distance, photorealistic, 3:2](/images/stories/czechs-rally-prague-public-media.webp)

Hundreds of thousands of people filled Letna park in Prague on Sunday to protest a government bill to abolish the household license fee that funds Czech Television and Czech Radio, in what organizers called the largest demonstration since the 1989 Velvet Revolution.

Mikulas Minar, head of the civic group Million Moments for Democracy, which organized the rally, said between 350,000 and 450,000 people came in the afternoon, later estimating the crowd at some 400,000. The group had drawn an estimated quarter of a million to each of three earlier rallies against Prime Minister Andrej Babis — two during his first government from 2017 to 2021 and one in March.

The bill comes from Mr. Babis's three-party coalition: his ANO party, the far-right Freedom and Direct Democracy party of Tomio Okamura, and the Motorists. It would cancel the 205-crown, roughly $9.26, monthly household broadcasting fee and fund the broadcasters from the state budget instead. Parliament passed the bill on a first reading in an overnight session that ended at 4:36 a.m. last month; the hour of the vote has become a second symbol of the protest. Two readings remain.

The overhaul would cut the broadcasters' financing by about 15 percent, reducing Czech Television's nominal budget to its 2008 level and Czech Radio's to 2005. The broadcasters say that is unworkable. Critics say budget funding would place the broadcasters at the mercy of whichever government holds the purse strings. "An elected government has the right to govern but it does not have the right to do whatever it wants and to dismantle democratic institutions," Mr. Minar said. The European Broadcasting Union, the European Federation of Journalists, Reporters Without Borders and the International Press Institute have objected to the bill.

Mr. Babis dismissed the objections last week. "Nobody wants to control public media, let alone nationalise them," he said. "None of what the people are protesting against ... is true." He has long accused Czech public and private media of bias.

Staff of the two public broadcasters staged a one-day strike on November 3, and thousands marched in other cities. The crowd wore triangular paper hats folded from newspapers, a protest symbol taken from a Czech Television animated character, and carried banners reading "we won't give media away." Mr. Minar presented a petition calling for the bill's withdrawal and for the removal of Mr. Okamura as parliament's speaker, accusing his "pro-Russian SPD" of endangering national security.

The rally was staged days before the November 17 public holiday that commemorates the Velvet Revolution. The bill remains before parliament, where the coalition holds the votes that carried it through the first reading.
