First story: The United States reportedly carried out limited strikes on Qeshm Island, an Iranian target in the Strait of Hormuz. Reuters and the Associated Press report the strikes hit defensive surface-to-air missile sites being used against American assets. If confirmed, it would be the first publicly known American strike on Iranian territory in weeks. The Wall Street Journal says the operation was part of a series of covert attacks aimed at deterring Tehran's assaults on commercial shipping. Residents on Qeshm heard explosions; Iranian authorities have not commented. Meanwhile, Vice-President J. D. Vance told Fox News he was optimistic about a deal, saying Iran wants to make a deal very badly and there is a very good chance one will happen. President Trump, asked how soon the war would end, said this isn't war, and that there hasn't been shooting for months. The reported strike came on the eve of the midterm elections.

Next: The link between diplomacy and the oil market. Brent crude fell more than six per cent, to one hundred five dollars and fifty-five cents a barrel, its lowest level in more than two weeks, after reports that Iran's supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, granted his government powers to negotiate an end to the war. Iran's foreign ministry said Tehran has a plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if talks progress. President Trump said he is in no rush to decide whether to resume attacks, while adding that the Iranian leadership wants a deal more than it ever has. Reporters say the White House is split into three camps: one favouring negotiations, one backing renewed hostilities, and one pressing continued economic pressure. Markets rallied on the prospect of cheaper fuel, with United Airlines jumping seven per cent in New York. Traders caution that the strait stays closed until there is a durable settlement, not just a mandate to talk.

Next: In Ukraine, Russian forces bombed a school being used as an aid distribution centre and shelter in the village of Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region. Regional officials put the death toll at at least twenty-nine as rescue operations continued, with ten people injured and forty-eight unaccounted for. At least nine three-tonne aerial bombs struck the village. Among the dead were two members of a rescue crew and four police officers. President Volodymyr Zelensky said Russian aircraft first dropped bombs on the school just as residents were arriving for their weekly humanitarian aid, then struck a second time on the place where people were sheltering. He called it a vile terrorist strike. Rescue efforts were hindered by further Russian drone attacks in the area. Zelensky said the attack shows Moscow is counting on further killings, coming after last week's joint humanitarian evacuation during a temporary ceasefire brokered by United States First Lady Melania Trump.

Next: Americans vote today in midterm elections that will decide control of both chambers of Congress, thirty-six governorships and thousands of state and local offices, and shape the final two years of President Donald Trump's second term. Every seat in the four hundred thirty-five member House is contested; Democrats need a net gain of four to take it. In the Senate, where about a third of the seats are up, Democrats again need a net gain of four, and a fifty-fifty split would leave Vice-President J. D. Vance holding the tie-breaking vote. A record three point eight billion dollars had been spent on political advertising by mid-October. The economy has dominated the closing weeks: a New York Federal Reserve study found tariffs had added two point nine percentage points to goods price inflation. Officials caution that results may not be known on election night, because close races decided by mail ballots could remain uncalled late into the week. The Michigan Senate race is among those that may decide control of the chamber.

Next: The Supreme Court has refused the Trump administration's emergency request to force California election officials to set aside mail ballots cast by some twenty thousand people the government alleges are likely non-citizens. The court issued a one-sentence, unsigned order with no explanation and no noted dissents. The administration had already lost at every lower level. A district judge in Los Angeles found the government's process for identifying alleged non-citizens rife with uncertainty and errors, and the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held that the Constitution reserves authority over elections to the states and Congress, not the executive. State officials said the government's lists appeared to be filled with errors that could disenfranchise legal voters. The Justice Department has separately brought criminal charges against more than two hundred individuals it alleges voted illegally in past elections. More than six point four million Californians had already voted as of Sunday. Governor Gavin Newsom responded to the order with a single line: Justice is served.

Next: A federal magistrate judge in Washington has dismissed the criminal case against two former F. B. I. counterintelligence officials, Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides, who were charged with lying to Congress about the bureau's Hunter Biden laptop investigation. Judge Zia Faruqui found no probable cause, ruling that some of the statements were not false and others could be attributed to honest mistakes of memory. From the bench he said: without motive, without direct evidence, I have nothing. He questioned why the case was brought three years after the testimony and less than two weeks before the midterm elections, asking whether there was any other event on the calendar except the midterms, and adding that what transpired cannot be described as anything other than weaponization. The ruling noted that the probe began after President Trump's primetime address on election security in July. A lawyer for Ms Floris said the case should never have been brought. The Justice Department disagrees and says it is evaluating all options.

Next: The crypto exchange Binance says it has replaced about ninety-five per cent of the assets stolen in last week's six point three billion dollar cyber-attack, the largest theft in cryptocurrency history. But customers withdrew a net three point six billion dollars on Sunday, the first day of reopened trading after a suspension of roughly forty-five hours. The theft itself took about twenty minutes, and early checks suggest the intrusion may have begun with a compromised laptop belonging to an engineer. Binance and Western agencies attribute the attack to a North Korean cybercrime group also known as the Lazarus Group. Pyongyang denies involvement. The F. B. I. has published a list of cryptocurrency wallet addresses linked to the group and asked industry partners to block related transactions. Binance, working with other exchanges, says it has frozen about three billion dollars of the stolen funds, and the company has reserved more than six point three billion dollars to cover customer losses. Some withdrawal restrictions remained in place as the phased reopening continued.

Next: Dozens of supermarkets across seven European countries have reported suspected cyber-attacks on their refrigeration systems overnight. Around forty Delhaize stores in Belgium and Luxembourg were affected, with smaller numbers of shops hit in Sweden, Ireland, the Netherlands, Estonia and Finland. The Belgian manufacturer Sabcobel said the systems' evaporators were switched to defrost mode, causing the cabinets to warm up and spoiling frozen stock. Investigators believe the method matched that used in an attack on hundreds of French supermarkets last month. Retail executives estimate losses from spoiled frozen food in the tens of millions of euros across the two incidents. No party has claimed responsibility, and no government has publicly attributed the attacks. The incident raises fresh questions about connected store equipment, because refrigeration and building-management systems often share networks with checkout and inventory software, and are frequently patched less rigorously than corporate systems.

Next: A child in rural northern New York has died of measles, the first measles-associated death in the state in more than thirty years. The fatal case was recorded in Lewis County, in the Adirondack region, which has reported fifteen cases in the ongoing outbreak. New York has recorded one hundred seventy-eight measles cases this year, the vast majority of them outside New York City. State health officials said they are deeply saddened by the death and asked that the family's privacy be respected. Governor Kathy Hochul, who declared a State Disaster Emergency four weeks ago, tied the resurgence to eroding vaccination levels, saying measles is highly contagious and vaccination remains our best protection. Public health guidance notes that two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine offer ninety-seven per cent protection. The New York outbreak runs alongside a larger one in neighbouring Pennsylvania, the country's worst in thirty years, with more than fourteen hundred cases and two hundred sixty-nine hospitalisations. The child's age and vaccination status were not disclosed.

Next: The Asian Football Confederation has postponed the twenty twenty-seven Asian Cup in Saudi Arabia, moving the tournament from its planned January window to December twenty twenty-seven, through January twenty twenty-eight, because of the conflicts engulfing the region. The decision follows a series of Houthi missile and drone attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities. A missile strike on Riyadh's international airport last month killed at least twelve people and wounded more than three hundred. The disruption reaches well beyond football. Golf's Asian Tour has postponed the Saudi International indefinitely, a world cycling championship was cancelled, an exhibition tennis tournament in Riyadh has been moved, and Fifa postponed its esports finals in Jeddah. For Saudi Arabia, which has invested heavily in professional sport and pitched the Asian Cup as an early rehearsal for staging the World Cup in twenty thirty-four, the rescheduling carries a symbolic cost. The new dates still need Fifa's approval, and the tournament will remain in Saudi Arabia.

Jeffery Simmons, traded to Chicago from the Tennessee Titans just days earlier, sacked Sam Darnold on Seattle's fourth play from scrimmage, finished with six tackles, and was given the game ball. The Bears' defence held Seattle to a single third-down conversion on eleven attempts. Caleb Williams completed twenty-four of thirty-four passes for two hundred forty-seven yards and one touchdown without a turnover, and D'Andre Swift scored three short touchdowns. The loss ended a remarkable streak: in thirty games across the regular season and playoffs, Seattle had never lost by more than four points. Williams said of his new defensive arrival: I'm happy he's on our side. He helps everybody on that field on the defense.
